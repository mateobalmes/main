The Brazil bridge

Full answer

The strongest bridge is Embraer. Air Canada Express flies the E175. The reason it's the E175 and not the newer E175-E2 is US scope clauses — pilot contracts capping regional aircraft weight and seats, which the E2 exceeds. The better one if the conversation goes there: the A220 Air Canada flies is built in Mirabel and started as the Bombardier C Series. Bombardier and Embraer fought for years, including Canada and Brazil taking each other to the WTO over aircraft subsidies. Today the Canadian plane is Airbus and Embraer stayed independent. A Canada–Brazil story with both of you in it.

Do not reference the GOL–Azul merger as live. Abra called it off in September 2025 and killed the codeshare with it. The safer Brazil observation: Brazil is the one major market where no carrier belongs to a global alliance — yet all three are wired into US airlines. LATAM through its Delta JV after leaving oneworld, GOL through Abra and American, Azul now through United and American equity. Alliance-free but not partner-free. Nice contrast with Air Canada, a founding Star Alliance member since 1997.

Boeing vs Airbus, if it comes up

  • control authority is the split
  • Airbus: hard envelope, sidesticks, static thrust levers
  • Boeing: pilot has final say, linked yokes, moving throttles
  • Airbus's real weapon is commonality
  • one type rating across the A320 family
Full answer

The philosophical split is control authority. Airbus fly-by-wire enforces a hard flight envelope — the aircraft won't let you exceed its limits. Boeing gives the pilot final authority with soft limits that can be overridden. Everything else follows: Airbus sidesticks aren't mechanically linked between pilots, Boeing yokes are; Airbus autothrust levers sit static in detents, Boeing autothrottles physically move.

Commercially, Airbus's real weapon is commonality — one type rating across the whole A320 family, cross-crew qualification up to the A330 and A350. A training-cost argument, which is how it wins fleet decisions.

Spotter details: the 737's flat-bottomed nacelles exist because it sits too low for a round one. The 787's raked wingtips and serrated chevrons are what make it quiet. The GE90 on a 777-300ER has a fan about as wide as a 737 fuselage. The A320's power transfer unit barks on the ground.

Air Canada spans both: 737 MAX 8, 777, 787 on the Boeing side; A220, A320 family, A330 on Airbus; E175s at Express.
