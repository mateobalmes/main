The angle

Read this once. Every answer in every stage follows from it.

The posting has a tell. They want a v7 to v8 upgrade led, and separately they want Journey Optimizer owned, Experience Platform audiences activated into both tools, and offer decisioning stood up. That is a shop with a legacy email platform and an ambition to run real-time orchestration on a CDP. The Campaign Classic requirement describes the work in front of them. The AJO and AEP requirements describe where they are trying to get.

Most candidates who clear the four-year Campaign bar will be deep in the legacy half and thin on the new half. You are the reverse, and the reverse is rarer. Better still, your orchestration experience is not one platform. It is Journey Optimizer at Tim Hortons, and before that Lytics, Braze and Segment on P&G programs at DXC. Braze is one of three platforms this posting names as an acceptable equivalent. So the honest count is roughly six years of orchestration and CDP work across four platforms, of which the Adobe-branded portion is the most recent three.

That reframes the gap entirely. You are not a candidate short on orchestration asking to learn it. You are a candidate long on orchestration who has not used one specific legacy build tool. Those are different conversations, and only one of them ends in an offer.

The sentence that carries the whole process

The Campaign Classic build mechanics I can be productive in within a quarter. The Experience Platform and Journey Optimizer judgment you need for the rest of this roadmap took me six years to build, and I already have it.

Your submitted CV states plainly that Campaign Classic is ecosystem exposure rather than production depth. That was the right call and it is worth a great deal, because it means every other claim you make is trusted on sight. Do not walk it back at any stage. Own it in one sentence, bridge, move on.
Two facts to have settled before stage one. What you have been doing since May 2026, said in one clean sentence. And whether the on-site location works, since Air Canada's Toronto base is at the airport rather than downtown.

What is actually happening

A recruiter cannot evaluate platform depth and will not try. They are checking boxes against a requisition and building a summary that gets passed to the hiring manager. That summary is usually three or four lines long, and it is written during your call.

So your real job is not to impress. It is to hand the recruiter a sentence about your Campaign Classic position that they can repeat accurately to someone else without garbling it. If you ramble, they will summarize it as "no Campaign experience" and you are done. If you give them a clean line, they will write down something closer to "AJO and AEP depth, Campaign through the Adobe relationship, has a ramp plan."

Say this early, unprompted

Before we go further, one thing I want to be straight about because it is on my CV. My production depth is Journey Optimizer and Experience Platform, not Campaign Classic. I have worked around Campaign through the Adobe vendor relationship and the shared data layer, but I have not built v7 workflows day to day. I would rather you hear that from me than find it later.

Where I think I am unusual is the other half of your posting. Three years running Journey Optimizer and Experience Platform in production for Tim Hortons across four thousand locations, and before that Braze, Lytics and Segment on P&G programs. Your posting names Braze as an acceptable equivalent. So the orchestration and CDP judgment is there. The Campaign build mechanics are what I would ramp on, and I would take certification on my own time.

  • Debug an audience mismatch. The CDP says one number, the send delivered another. Answer with the order you check, not a list of causes: segment definition, ingestion and dataflow latency, identity and profile merge, consent and suppression, then delivery-level exclusions.
  • Schema mapping. How XDM maps onto Campaign Classic's relational model for profile merging. You have done the XDM half end to end. Be explicit that the Campaign relational side is the part you would learn, then reason about it out loud anyway. Reasoning honestly about something you have not done reads far better than bluffing.
  • Batch versus triggered standards. What belongs in Campaign and what belongs in AJO. A judgment question, not a knowledge one. Batch, high-volume, scheduled, heavy targeting logic sits in Campaign. Event-driven, real-time, individually triggered, decisioning-dependent sits in AJO. The interesting part is the grey zone and how you would rule on it.

Where you are genuinely exposed

Campaign-specific mechanics: typology rules, Message Center patterns, delivery templates, the v7 workflow canvas, and Campaign's own SQL targeting syntax. Do not attempt these from first principles in the room. The right move is to say what you do not know, then map it to the equivalent you do know and ask them how it works there. Curiosity in a technical interview reads as competence; guessing reads as risk.

You are not trying to fake four years. You are trying to hold a fluent conversation and ask good questions, which is achievable in an hour and will be noticed.

What is actually happening

Their posting names the steering forum explicitly: Marketing, Data, IT, Privacy and executives. Each seat wants something different from this role, and the candidate who gives all four the same answer loses to the one who reads the room.

What each seat wants to hear

  • Marketing. Faster launches and campaigns that do not need a ticket. Talk time to launch, reusable templates, self-serve.
  • Data. That you will not treat the CDP as a black box. Talk schemas, contracts, reconciliation, and that you write your own SQL.
  • IT. That you will define latency expectations and dependencies rather than discovering them in production. Talk data contracts and documentation.
  • Privacy. That consent is enforced upstream in the data layer where it cannot be bypassed, not bolted onto the send. This is your single strongest sentence with this seat.

If they ask for a ninety-day plan or a platform assessment

Structure it as assessment, then stabilization, then roadmap. Never present a roadmap without an assessment in front of it, because a roadmap written before you have seen the platform tells them you would do the same thing on the job.

  • Days 1 to 30, learn and inventory. Platform configuration as built, what is undocumented, the dataflows and where they break, and a conversation with every Marketing Operations person about their top three frustrations.
  • Days 31 to 60, stabilize. Verify consent and suppression enforcement in both tools rather than assuming it. Document what only one person knows. Sequence the migration with cutover risks named.
  • Days 61 to 90, commit. A roadmap with the KPI baseline measured, the standards for batch versus triggered agreed, and the first reusable template patterns in Marketing Operations' hands.

Deliberately change little in the first thirty days and say so. Restraint reads as senior.

If you present, use the format you already know from monthly search performance reporting to VP level at Restaurant Brands: finding first, evidence under it, recommendation at the end. Slide titles should be findings rather than topics.

Stage 6 · Offer and negotiation

Handled badly, this is where people lose money they already earned.

Before any number is discussed

  • Ask their budgeted range first, at every stage where it comes up. The first number spoken anchors the rest.
  • If pushed, give a band rather than a point, and say the band is for total package rather than base.
  • Know your floor before the first call and do not move it because a conversation went well.
  • This is a specialist platform role with a named migration attached, which typically pays above a generalist product manager band.

Levers beyond base

  • Bonus target and how it is measured
  • Travel benefits, which at an airline are a real and underrated part of the package
  • Adobe certification sponsorship, which you should ask for anyway because it converts your one gap into their investment
  • Start date, and days on-site in practice
Do not negotiate against a number you have not received in writing, and do not accept in the same conversation. Thank them, ask for it in writing, and give a date by which you will respond.

Success in role

They published their own KPI list, which is unusual and useful. Say it back with a view on sequence.

Their five measures

  • Time to launch. Request to send. Falls when templates and workflow patterns are reusable, rises when every campaign is bespoke.
  • Workflow reuse. Share of sends built from standard patterns. The clearest single proxy for whether the platform work is landing.
  • Deliverability health. Reputation, bounce and complaint rates, and whether warm-up and suppression are holding.
  • Journey throughput. Volume and reliability of triggered and real-time journeys, which only grows once the event and audience plumbing is trustworthy.
  • Marketing Operations self-serve maturity. How much they can do without you. This determines whether the role is strategic or a service desk.

What good looks like

Ninety days. You know the platform including what is undocumented. The migration is assessed and sequenced with cutover risks named. You have met every Marketing Operations person and know their top three frustrations. You have deliberately changed little.

Six months. The upgrade is through cutover and in hypercare with no material send incidents. Workflow templates and targeting standards exist and are in use. Consent and audience enforcement from the CDP is verified in both tools rather than assumed.

Twelve months. Time to launch measurably down, reuse is the default, journeys run at volume with offer decisioning live, and Marketing Operations builds most of what it needs without escalating. The platform is documented well enough that your absence would not stop it.

If asked which KPI you would fix first, do not name a metric. Give the order: reliability and consent enforcement first because everything downstream depends on it, then reuse and time to launch, then throughput and self-serve. Sequence reads as judgment; a favourite metric reads as a guess.

Glossary

Every platform and term in the posting, explained plainly, with an example and where you have touched it. Entries with a coloured edge are ones you should be able to explain without hesitating.

THE TWO PLATFORMS THIS ROLE OWNS

Adobe Campaign Classic

Their main email platform. Old, powerful, built for big scheduled sends.

Campaign Classic is Adobe's long-standing email and direct marketing tool. You build a workflow, which is a flowchart of steps: pull an audience, filter it, split it, wait, then send. It sits on its own relational database, meaning customer data lives in tables with columns and keys, much like a traditional SQL database. That design is why it handles enormous scheduled sends well and why it feels dated compared with newer tools.

Version 7 is the older architecture. Version 8 moves the data layer onto a cloud database and changes how some things work under the hood. Their posting says a v7 to v8 upgrade is coming, which tells you they are on the old one now.

Example. A monthly statement email to eight million customers. A workflow queries the database for eligible customers, removes anyone who unsubscribed, splits them into English and French, personalizes each message, and sends overnight.

You. This is the declared gap. You have worked around it through the Adobe vendor relationship and the shared data layer, not inside it day to day.

Adobe Journey Optimizer (AJO)

The modern counterpart. Reacts to individual customers in real time.

Where Campaign asks "who should get this send tonight," Journey Optimizer asks "what should happen for this person right now." You build a journey, which listens for an event, then branches based on what the customer does next. It runs on top of Experience Platform, so it sees a live customer profile rather than a nightly extract.

Example. Someone abandons a booking. That fires an event. The journey waits two hours, checks whether they came back, and if not sends a reminder with the fare they were looking at. If they did come back, they exit the journey and get nothing.

You. Three years running this in production for Tim Hortons across 4,000 locations. Journey design, event configuration, trigger setup, audience targeting, traffic splits, real-time use cases and A/B testing. This is your strongest ground in the entire posting.

THE DATA LAYER UNDERNEATH BOTH

Customer Data Platform (CDP), and Adobe's Real-Time CDP

The single place where all customer data is unified into one profile per person.

A CDP takes data from everywhere the company touches a customer, the app, the website, purchases, the loyalty program, the call centre, and stitches it into one profile per human being. Then it hands audiences built from those profiles to the tools that actually send things. Adobe's version is Real-Time CDP, part of Adobe Experience Platform.

The reason it matters here is in their posting: audiences and consent should come from the CDP and be enforced before anything reaches Campaign or AJO. Without that, each send tool keeps its own version of the truth and they drift apart.

Example. One person has a mobile app login, a web cookie, a loyalty number and an email address. The CDP recognizes all four as the same human, so when you build the audience "flew in the last 90 days but has not opened the app," you get one entry for them, not four.

You. Configured Adobe Experience Platform end to end at RBI: Real-Time CDP audience segmentation, data ingestion, XDM schema alignment, and activation dataflows out to downstream channels. You also worked with Segment and Lytics at DXC, which are the same category of tool.

XDM schema

Adobe's standard vocabulary for customer data. A schema is the shape of the data.

XDM stands for Experience Data Model. A schema defines what fields exist and what type each one is: email is text, birth date is a date, loyalty tier is one of a fixed list. Every system feeding the platform has to map its own field names onto this shared vocabulary, otherwise nothing lines up.

This is where the interesting half of the requirement sits. Their posting asks you to align XDM schemas with Campaign Classic's relational data model so profiles merge correctly. XDM is a nested, document-like structure; Campaign's is flat tables. Getting those two to agree on who a person is, is genuinely fiddly work.

Example. The booking system calls it cust_email, the loyalty system calls it member_email_address, and the app calls it contactEmail. All three map to the same XDM field so the platform knows they refer to one thing.

You. XDM schema alignment is a configured responsibility on your CV, not exposure. The Campaign relational side is the half you would learn, and saying that plainly is stronger than bluffing it.

Identity resolution and profile merge

Deciding that these five records are all the same person, and which value wins when they disagree.

Identity resolution links records across systems using shared keys such as email, loyalty number or device ID. Profile merge, governed by merge policies, decides what happens when two linked records disagree, for instance two different mailing addresses. Usually the most recent wins, but you choose that rule deliberately.

Most audience count mysteries trace back here. If identity is too loose, you merge two different people. Too strict, and one person shows up as three, inflating your audience and sending them the same message three times.

Example. Someone books as a guest with a personal email, then later logs into their loyalty account with a work email. Identity resolution links them by phone number, and the merge policy decides which email is the one you actually send to.

You. Squarely inside the AEP work you configured. If the technical round goes here, you are on home ground.

Data ingestion and activation dataflows

Ingestion is data coming in. Activation is audiences going back out to the send tools.

Ingestion is how data reaches the platform, either in batch, meaning a scheduled file, or streaming, meaning near-instant. Activation is the return trip: an audience built in the CDP is pushed out to Campaign, AJO, an ad platform, wherever it is needed. The dataflow is the configured pipe that carries it, with a schedule, a mapping and a failure behaviour.

Their posting asks you to architect and verify these flows and to define data contracts and latency expectations with IT and vendors. The word verify is doing real work there. Plenty of teams assume a dataflow is running correctly until an audience arrives empty.

Example. Yesterday's flights ingest overnight in batch. App activity streams in within seconds. An audience of "delayed over three hours yesterday" activates into AJO each morning so an apology journey can run.

You. Activation dataflows from CDP to downstream channels is a CV line. This is one of the strongest overlaps you have with their cross-platform section.

Data contract and latency

A written agreement about what data arrives, how fresh it is, and what happens when it breaks.

A data contract specifies the fields and their types, how often data arrives, how much volume to expect, what happens on failure, and who gets paged. Latency is the delay between something happening in the real world and the platform knowing about it. The part teams usually forget is failure behaviour, so lead with it when asked.

Example. "Booking events arrive within five minutes, ninety-nine percent of the time. If the feed stops for more than fifteen minutes, journeys depending on it pause rather than firing on stale data, and IT is alerted."

Workflow, delivery, template (Campaign vocabulary)

The three nouns Campaign people use constantly. Worth being fluent in even without hands-on hours.

A template is a reusable starting point so nobody rebuilds the same structure from scratch. Typology and typology rules

Campaign's built-in guardrails. The rules that quietly stop bad sends before they go.

A typology is a set of rules applied to every delivery automatically: fatigue rules capping how many emails one person gets per week, consent and pressure rules, content checks, deliverability checks. It is the safety net that means an individual campaign builder cannot accidentally email someone eleven times in a day.

Example. A rule saying no customer receives more than three promotional emails per week. A marketer builds a campaign targeting everyone, and the typology silently removes anyone already at their limit.

Journey, event, trigger, traffic split (AJO vocabulary)

Your vocabulary. Use these nouns freely, because they prove you have actually built.

A journey is the flow a single customer moves through. An event is something that happened, such as a booking or a check-in, arriving as a message the platform listens for. A trigger is the condition that starts a journey. A traffic split divides people down different paths, either to test two versions or to hold back a control group.

Example. Check-in opens twenty-four hours before departure and fires an event. That triggers a journey. A traffic split sends half the travellers a seat-upgrade offer and half a plain reminder, so you can measure whether the offer changes behaviour.

Offer decisioning and AJO Offers

Letting the system choose which offer a person sees, rather than hard-coding it.

The part of Campaign that sends transactional messages, one at a time, instantly.

Transactional means triggered by something the customer did and expected by them: a booking confirmation, a password reset, a receipt. CONSENT AND COMPLIANCE

Consent

Permission to contact someone, recorded, with proof of when and how you got it.

Express consent means the person actively agreed, ticking a box or signing up. Implied consent covers a narrower set of situations, such as an existing business relationship, and it expires. The key operational point is that consent is a record you must be able to produce, not a setting.

Example. Someone ticks a marketing box at booking. You store what they agreed to, the exact wording shown, the timestamp and the source. Two years later a regulator asks, and you can produce it.

You. Consent and suppression enforcement before sending, across all activation touchpoints, is a CV line. This is a strong area for you.

Suppression

The list of people who must not receive this, applied before the send, not after.

Suppression covers unsubscribes, hard bounces, complainers and legal exclusions. The judgment question their posting is really asking is where you apply it. Apply it in the data layer and it holds everywhere automatically. Apply it inside each campaign and every new channel has to re-implement it, and eventually one of them gets it wrong.

Example. Someone unsubscribes on Monday. Because suppression sits in the CDP rather than in each tool, they are already excluded from Tuesday's Campaign send and Wednesday's AJO journey without anyone doing anything.

You. This is your executive-round argument, and it is true, which is why it will hold up under questioning.

CASL, and GDPR

CASL is Canada's anti-spam law. GDPR is Europe's privacy regulation.

CASL requires consent before commercial electronic messages, clear sender identification, a working unsubscribe honoured within ten business days, and records proving consent. Penalties are substantial and it applies to messages sent to Canadians. Air Canada is federally regulated and Canadian, so CASL is a daily operating constraint rather than a legal footnote.

GDPR is broader, covering data rights generally: access, deletion, portability, and a lawful basis for processing. If you handled European customer data it applied to you.

Example. Under CASL, someone unsubscribing from promotional email must stop receiving it within ten business days, though you may still send their booking confirmation, because that is transactional rather than commercial.

You. Tim Hortons is Canadian, so CASL governed your work. At DXC you ran full GDPR compliance on the P&G platform builds. You can speak to both.

DELIVERABILITY

Deliverability

Whether your email actually reaches the inbox rather than spam or nowhere.

Mailbox providers such as Gmail and Outlook decide whether to accept your mail based on your sending reputation. Reputation is built from how people react: opens and clicks help, spam complaints and sending to dead addresses hurt. Deliverability is not one setting, it is the accumulated consequence of how you send.

Example. A team buys a list and sends to it. Complaints spike, Gmail starts filtering, and suddenly the regular newsletter that was fine last month lands in spam for everyone.

You. You were accountable for platform reliability at 4,000 locations. Talk about it as one system rather than reciting definitions.

IP and domain warm-up

Building sending reputation gradually instead of blasting from a cold start.

A brand-new sending IP address or domain has no history, and mailbox providers treat unknown senders that suddenly send millions of messages as spam. Warm-up means starting small, sending to your most engaged people first, and increasing volume over days or weeks while reputation builds.

This matters directly for their migration. A v7 to v8 move can change sending infrastructure, and mishandling warm-up at cutover is one of the classic ways a migration visibly fails.

Example. Day one, fifty thousand to people who opened something in the last thirty days. Day three, two hundred thousand. Two weeks later, the full file.

You. Worth raising unprompted in the migration answer. Naming warm-up as a cutover risk signals you have thought about how migrations actually break.

Bounces, complaints, and authentication

The signals that make or break reputation, and the records that prove you are you.

A hard bounce is a permanent failure, usually a dead address, and must be suppressed immediately. A soft bounce is temporary, such as a full mailbox, and can be retried. A complaint is someone hitting the spam button, and it is the most damaging signal of all. SPF, DKIM and DMARC are DNS records that prove your mail genuinely comes from your domain, and without them modern providers will not trust you.

Example. A list has not been cleaned in a year. Ten percent hard bounce on the first send, reputation drops sharply, and the next campaign underperforms for reasons that have nothing to do with its content.

You. Know these cold, but do not recite them. The better answer describes what you did when reputation moved.

THE MIGRATION

Their exact wording. Use their phases back at them, because it shows you read the posting.
