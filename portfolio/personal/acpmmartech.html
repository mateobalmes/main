Platform reference

For the hiring manager round more than this one. Skim it once so nothing here is a surprise word.

Adobe Campaign Classic

Batch campaign and email platform — the job's core requirement

What it is

Adobe's long-standing campaign management and email platform. Older and more technical than most modern tools. Operated largely through a thick client console, with a relational data model underneath rather than the schema-based model AEP uses.

What it does

  • Workflows — visual drag-and-drop chains of activities: query, split, deduplication, enrichment, wait, delivery, JavaScript code. This is where the actual work happens.
  • Deliveries — the sends themselves, built from templates with personalization blocks.
  • Targeting — queries against the relational model, or raw SQL when the query builder isn't enough.
  • Typologies — rule sets applied to every delivery. Filtering rules exclude people, pressure or fatigue rules cap frequency, control rules validate before send, capacity rules protect throughput.
  • Message Center — the transactional messaging module. See its own entry below.
  • Scripting — JavaScript inside workflow activities and personalization. The posting asks for this.

Martech use cases

  • Weekly batch newsletters to a large list with frequency capping across programs
  • Recurring workflows that pull an audience, dedupe, split for A/B, and send on a schedule
  • Suppression and bounce management maintained centrally so every campaign inherits it
  • Reusable delivery templates so operations teams launch without rebuilding

Worth knowing if they dig

  • Seed addresses and proofs — test records inserted into a send for verification
  • The recipient, delivery and broad log tables are the core of the data model
  • It is not real-time. Batch is its native mode, which is exactly why AJO exists alongside it.

Campaign v7 to v8

The migration in the posting

What changes

v7 runs on a traditional relational database — Oracle, PostgreSQL or SQL Server, often hosted or on-premise. v8 re-platforms onto a cloud database built on Snowflake, with a local database kept for transient and staging data. Adobe calls the architecture full federated data access.

Why it's more than an upgrade

  • Writes to the cloud database go through a staging mechanism rather than directly, so old workflow patterns often need refactoring
  • Custom schemas and custom code need review rather than lift-and-shift
  • Scale improves substantially, which is usually the business reason for doing it

Your honest angle

Adobe publishes the upgrade path and the compatibility checklist. The technical route is documented. What decides the outcome is sequencing and cross-team coordination — IT, data, privacy, deliverability, and the operations team who have to keep sending throughout. That's the part you've done on a different migration.

Adobe Journey Optimizer

Real-time orchestration — your strongest ground

What it is

Adobe's journey and messaging application, built natively on Experience Platform. Where Campaign is batch-first, AJO is event-first and real-time.

Deliverability and CASL

Concepts, not tools

Deliverability

  • Authentication — SPF, DKIM and DMARC prove you're allowed to send as that domain
  • IP warm-up — volume ramped gradually on a new sending IP so receivers build trust
  • Hard versus soft bounces — permanent failures suppressed immediately, temporary ones retried
  • Suppression lists — unsubscribes, complaints and hard bounces, enforced before every send
  • Reputation — sender score, complaint rate, spam trap hits

CASL

  • Express consent — actively given, doesn't expire until withdrawn
  • Implied consent — from an existing business relationship, time-limited
  • Records of consent must be kept and produced on request
  • Unsubscribe must work within ten business days and stay valid for sixty
  • Penalties are significant, which is why the posting says CASL-aware rather than CASL-familiar

Refresher

Terms glossary

The words in the posting that carry weight. Dashed underlines in the answers above show these on hover and link down here.

Suppression

The list of people you must not email: unsubscribes, complaints, hard bounces, and anyone who has withdrawn consent. In practice it is a table checked automatically before every send, so no individual campaign can accidentally include them. Enforced at the audience layer rather than per tool, it only has to be right once.

Express versus implied consent

Express consent is actively given, by a tickbox or a signup, and lasts until the person withdraws it. Implied consent comes from an existing business relationship and expires, generally two years after a purchase or six months after an enquiry. Under CASL you have to be able to produce the record proving which one you hold.

Typologies

Campaign's rule sets, applied to every delivery rather than configured per campaign. Filtering rules exclude people. Pressure or fatigue rules cap how often one person can be contacted across all programs. Control rules validate a delivery before it goes. Capacity rules protect throughput. This is how a platform team enforces standards without policing each campaign.

Workflow

In Campaign, a visual chain of activities on a canvas: query an audience, split it, deduplicate, enrich, wait, then deliver. It is where the campaign logic actually lives. Reusable workflow templates are what let operations launch without rebuilding, which is why workflow reuse is one of the posting's KPIs.

IP warm-up

Sending volume increased gradually on a new or dormant sending IP so mailbox providers build trust in it. Skip it and a first large send gets throttled or filtered to spam. Comes up during any migration, because a new platform often means new sending infrastructure.

Hard and soft bounces

A hard bounce is a permanent failure, like an address that no longer exists, and gets suppressed immediately. A soft bounce is temporary, like a full mailbox or a server timeout, and is retried a set number of times before being treated as permanent. Failing to suppress hard bounces destroys sender reputation quickly.

XDM schema

Experience Data Model, Adobe's standard structure for customer data in Experience Platform. Looks like a defined field tree built from a class plus field groups, and every incoming dataset gets mapped into it. Campaign Classic instead uses a traditional relational model, which is precisely the reconciliation the posting asks about.

Merge policies

The rules deciding which source wins when systems disagree about the same person. If the loyalty database and the mobile app both hold an email address, the merge policy decides which one the unified profile shows. Get these wrong and audiences look right while messaging goes to the wrong address.

Identity graph

The map linking one person's identifiers together, such as email, loyalty number, device ID and cookie, so a single human is not treated as four separate customers. Underpins both audience accuracy and suppression, because you can only suppress someone reliably if you know all their identities.

Data contract

A written agreement between teams on exactly what data arrives, in what shape, how often, and who is accountable when it breaks. Prevents the upstream team renaming a field and silently breaking a live send. The posting asks you to define these with IT and vendors.

Latency

The gap between a customer doing something and the message reaching them. A batch newsletter tolerates hours. An abandoned booking trigger has minutes. Agreeing the expectation per use case is what stops the business asking for real-time on everything.

Throughput

How many messages a journey or delivery can push per unit of time before it queues. Matters most when one event qualifies a very large audience at once, which for an airline is a mass delay or cancellation.

Time to launch

How long from a campaign being requested to it actually sending. The first adoption KPI in the posting, because it measures whether the platform helps operations or obstructs them. Improved with templates and standards, not with more platform staff.

Key-person risk

When only one person understands how something is configured, so the platform stalls when they are away or leave. Reduced with documentation, runbooks and shared ownership of configuration. Named directly in the posting, which usually means they are living with it.

Self-serve maturity

How much routine work Marketing Operations can complete without raising a Martech ticket. High maturity means reusable templates, clear targeting standards and good documentation, so the platform team handles exceptions instead of everyday sends.

Runbook

A step-by-step operating document for a recurring task or a failure scenario, written so someone who has never done it can follow it under pressure. The practical form that reducing key-person risk takes.

Cutover

The moment live traffic switches from the old platform to the new one. Planned to the hour, usually with a campaign freeze around it and a documented rollback if things go wrong.

Hypercare

The heightened-support window immediately after a cutover. Extra monitoring, a standing war room and fast escalation, typically for a few weeks until the new platform is behaving normally. The posting lists it as a distinct migration phase, so treat it as one.
